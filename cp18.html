19  The Situation Model

19.1 How to Build a Structured Model of Context, Norms, Risk, Medium, Audience, and Constraints

A situation can be read, but it can also be modeled. Reading a situation means sensing what is happening in the field. Modeling means turning that perception into a structured representation that can be analyzed, shared, and used for decision-making. Without a model, people rely only on instinct. In simple situations, instinct may be enough. But in complex, high-stakes, hierarchical, public, cross-cultural, institutional, or emotionally charged situations, instinct is often insufficient. A person may misread silence as agreement, politeness as trust, directness as rudeness, or public behavior as sincere inner truth.

The situation model is a diagnostic tool that prevents these errors. It slows interpretation, forces systematic inquiry, and makes visible what would otherwise remain hidden. This chapter gathers the entire volume into an operational framework. It presents the twelve layers of the situation model, demonstrates how to apply it, and shows how modeling leads to better action — and to the higher skill of field design. By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to construct a situation model for any significant interaction and use it to guide interpretation and action.

19.1.1 Why a Situation Model Is Needed

Human beings explain behavior too quickly. They say: he is rude, she is cold, they are fake, he is weak, she is arrogant, they agreed, he refused, she overreacted. Sometimes these judgments are correct. But often they are incomplete because they ignore the situation. A person may seem rude because they come from a more direct communication field. They may seem cold because the medium removes warmth. They may seem fake because they are performing a role required by an institution. They may seem weak because hierarchy makes direct speech dangerous. They may seem arrogant because their confidence is read through status expectations. A group may seem aligned because disagreement is unsafe.

The situation model does not remove moral judgment; it improves it. It asks us to interpret behavior through the field that makes behavior meaningful. The question is not only “What did this person do?” but “What did this action become inside this situation?” The model provides a systematic way to answer that question.

The core principle of the situation model is: behavior = person × field. A person’s inner structure — values, motives, fears, habits, identity, temperament, skills, intentions — matters deeply. But behavior is not produced by inner structure alone; it is shaped by the field. The same person behaves differently across situations. The situation model therefore does not ask us to ignore the person. It asks us to understand the person inside the field.

19.1.2 The Twelve Layers of the Situation Model

A complete situation model contains twelve layers. Each layer corresponds to a dimension of the field studied in this volume.

Layer Core Question Key Concepts
1. Scene What is happening right now? Immediate interactional moment, mood, participants, activity
2. Occasion What kind of event is this? Social type of interaction, expected script
3. Setting What larger environment holds this? Physical, social, formal, symbolic dimensions
4. Norms What standards govern behavior here? Acceptability, appropriateness, official vs real norms
5. Scripts What pattern is being followed? Expected sequence, roles, official vs hidden scripts
6. Culture What interpretive background is active? Assumptions about self, relationship, authority, directness, face, etc.
7. Status hierarchy What is the power structure? Rank, age, wealth, gender, expertise — who has weight
8. Institution What institutional structures are active? Roles, categories, procedures, records
9. Rules and constraints What formal and informal rules shape the field? Written rules, hidden constraints, who cannot lose face
10. Stakes and risk What can be gained, lost, exposed, or damaged? Consequence structure, asymmetry of risk
11. Medium and channel Through what channel is interaction happening? Signal density, ambiguity, permanence, recordability
12. Audience and third parties Who is watching, and what roles do they play? Witnesses, rivals, allies, gatekeepers, imagined audience

These twelve layers form a complete diagnostic framework. Not every layer is equally important in every situation — a casual conversation between friends may require less attention to institutional structures, while a court hearing demands careful analysis of all twelve. The skill lies in knowing which layers are most relevant to the situation at hand.

19.1.3 Applying the Model

The situation model is applied through five diagnostic questions that organize the twelve layers into a practical workflow.

First: What is happening here? This identifies the scene, occasion, and setting (layers 1-3). It asks: What kind of interaction is this, where is it happening, and what kind of moment is it? This establishes the basic character of the field.

Second: What structures are shaping behavior? This identifies the norms, scripts, culture, status hierarchy, institutional structures, and rules (layers 4-9). It asks: What expectations, patterns, background assumptions, power relations, institutional forces, and constraints are active? This is the heart of the analysis — making visible the structures that are shaping behavior beneath the surface.

Third: What are the stakes and risks? This identifies the consequence structure (layer 10). It asks: What can be gained, lost, exposed, damaged, or made irreversible? Who bears the risk? Is there asymmetry? What would happen if the worst outcome occurred?

Fourth: What is the medium, and who is the audience? This identifies the channel and third-party field (layers 11-12). It asks: Through what channel is this interaction happening, and what does the channel add or remove? Who is watching, who may watch later, and what roles do third parties play?

Fifth: What action fits this field? This synthesizes the analysis into action. It asks: What kind of response is appropriate here? Should I speak or remain silent? Should I be direct or indirect? Should this conversation happen in private or public? Should I change the channel, bring a witness, or address the power structure? What would be wise here?

The five diagnostic questions can be used as a rapid mental checklist before any significant interaction. With practice, they become automatic. In complex situations, they can be written down and shared with trusted colleagues or allies to build a shared understanding of the field.

19.1.4 From Model to Action: Field Design

The situation model is not only for interpretation. It is also for action — and ultimately for field design. Field design means changing the conditions under which interaction happens to make it more truthful, safer, fairer, or more effective.

A person who can model a situation can also ask: What would happen if I changed one or more layers of this field? Moving a conversation from public to private changes the audience layer. Changing from text to face-to-face changes the channel layer. Bringing a witness changes the third-party layer. Making a hidden rule explicit changes the constraint layer. Naming a norm that was previously invisible changes the norm layer. Choosing a different occasion — reframing a performance review as a developmental conversation — changes the occasion layer.

Field design is the highest form of situational intelligence. It moves from passive reading to active construction. It asks not only “What field am I in?” but “What field should be created?” A serious apology needs privacy, time, and emotional presence — the wise person designs that field. A public harm may need public acknowledgment — the ethical person creates that field. A complex disagreement needs a smaller audience and a channel that allows repair — the skilled person constructs that field.

The twelve-layer model provides a systematic way to think about field design. For each layer, ask: Is this layer serving the interaction I need to have? If not, how can I change it? The ability to design fields — to reshape the conditions under which people interact — is one of the most powerful and responsible skills a person can develop.

19.1.5 Chapter Summary

The situation model integrates the entire volume into a practical tool. The core knowledge is condensed below.

Diagnostic Step Layers Analyzed Key Question
What is happening here? Scene, occasion, setting What kind of field is this?
What structures shape behavior? Norms, scripts, culture, status, institution, rules What invisible forces are active?
What are the stakes and risks? Stakes, risk, exposure What can be gained or lost?
What is the medium and audience? Channel, third parties Through what channel and before whom?
What action fits this field? All layers synthesized What would be wise here?

The twelve-layer model is a diagnostic tool, not a replacement for human judgment. Its purpose is to make the invisible visible, to slow down premature interpretation, and to provide a shared language for analyzing interaction. With practice, the layers become second nature — a habit of seeing the field before acting within it.

The ultimate goal of the situation model is not analysis for its own sake. It is wiser, more ethical, and more effective action. It is knowing when to speak and when to remain silent. When to be direct and when to be indirect. When to move public and when to move private. When to challenge a norm and when to follow it. When to bring a witness and when to protect privacy. When to follow procedure and when to set it aside. When to accept the field as given and when to redesign it.

Interaction = person × field. The person brings intention, character, and agency. The field shapes meaning, cost, and possibility. The situation model is the tool for reading the field. The rest is judgment, courage, and wisdom.
